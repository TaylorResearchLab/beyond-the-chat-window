## Related AI systems and scope of this work

Contemporary multi-agent frameworks provide concrete precedents for role assignment, shared artifacts, and review. Wu and colleagues' AutoGen supports conversational agents backed by language models, human input, and tools, with programmable interaction patterns and dynamic conversation flow [@arxiv:2308.08155]. Hong and colleagues' MetaGPT uses specialized roles, standard operating procedures, structured intermediate outputs, and a shared message pool for software development [@arxiv:2308.00352]. Qian and colleagues' ChatDev organizes software development through role-based communication across design, coding, testing, and documentation [@arxiv:2307.07924]. More directly, Salemi and colleagues describe an LLM-based blackboard system for data-science information discovery in which specialized agents coordinate through shared requests and responses [@arxiv:2510.01285]. These systems should not be characterized collectively as fixed, human-free pipelines: AutoGen explicitly includes human participation, and a blackboard can support opportunistic rather than predetermined coordination.

Scientific systems likewise combine agents, research tools, and human input in different ways. Swanson and colleagues' Virtual Lab describes meetings between an AI principal investigator and specialist agents with high-level feedback from a human researcher [@doi:10.1038/s41586-025-09442-9]. Ghareeb and colleagues' Robin links literature-driven reasoning and experimental feedback in a semi-autonomous discovery system [@arxiv:2505.13400]. Pantiukhin and colleagues describe a multi-agent system integrating Earth-science data access and analytical tools [@arxiv:2503.05854]. Shao and colleagues' SciSciGPT uses a ResearchManager agent to coordinate literature, database, analytics, and evaluation specialists, while researchers refine questions through a conversational interface [@doi:10.1038/s43588-025-00906-6]. The relevant distinction is how coordination and acceptance are organized, not whether AI can contribute to science or whether a human can participate.

Persistent scientific context and explicit governance are also addressed in recent preprints. Choudhury and colleagues' Mycelium represents observations, interpretations, hypotheses, and their provenance in an active shared context graph. Its runtime routes context across researchers' AI sessions and supports autonomous analytical work [@arxiv:2607.13220]. Kotalwar, Das, and Rosé examine shared group memory and selective approval gates for collaborative data analysis; their evaluated collaborators are simulated with language models rather than participating human investigators [@arxiv:2606.18413]. Version 1 of Weidener and colleagues' ClawdLab preprint describes role restrictions, versioned laboratory state, structured critique, and tool-linked evidence requirements enforced through a research protocol [@arxiv:2602.19810v1]. In that design, agents poll for tasks and an AI principal investigator manages state transitions and voting. These works provide direct precedents for shared context, procedural constraints, and evidence-linked collaboration. Their reported architectures and evaluations are not equivalent to the User-mediated implementation studied here.

Against this background, the present contribution is a documented operating workflow and adaptation materials, not a claim to originate human oversight, shared memory, provenance, or multi-agent review. The implementation developed through one investigator's coordination of ChatGPT, referred to here as GPT, and Claude. The assistants exchanged work asynchronously through a structured Notion Collaboration Log; GitHub held versioned artifacts, and final execution remained in a User-controlled computing environment. The User prompted each transition, selected task-relevant context, evaluated evidence, and determined the accepted project state. Provider diversity was a design choice rather than a demonstrated source of independent judgments or superior performance.

To support adoption, the main text explains how to establish the record, assign bounded work, obtain review, accept results, and resume across sessions. Supplementary Note S1 supplies the work contract, setup guide, schemas, and templates. SCUTER (Scientific Collaboration for User-directed, Traceable, Evidence-based Research) provides the protocol and adoption materials as a development-stage Skill and complete Markdown instructions. The retrospective account documents the workflow's use and the failures that informed these materials, separating the frozen single-investigator corpus from later manuscript development. It does not establish causal benefit or general usability. The current implementation guidance, historical observations, and proposed adaptations remain distinct throughout the paper.
