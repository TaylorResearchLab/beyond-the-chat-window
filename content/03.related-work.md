# Related work

The idea of independent knowledge sources contributing to a shared, structured workspace under a control regime is not new. Blackboard architectures were developed for exactly this shape of problem, most influentially in Hearsay-II [@doi:10.1145/356810.356816], and the model was later generalized and surveyed as a framework for problem solving [@doi:10.1609/aimag.v7i2.537]. The workflow described here is recognizably a blackboard: Notion is the blackboard, the two assistants are knowledge sources, and the User is the control component that decides which source acts next and whether its contribution enters accepted state.

What differs is not the topology but the setting. Classical blackboard systems were single programs with a scheduler; the knowledge sources were components of one artifact under one owner. Here the knowledge sources are commercial services from competing vendors, with no shared memory, no shared context, no message channel, and no common execution environment. The control component is a person rather than a scheduler, and the blackboard is a commercial productivity database rather than a data structure. The contribution is therefore not the architecture but the demonstration that it can be assembled from tools a working scientist already has, and that the resulting record is durable enough to audit.

Contemporary multi-agent frameworks automate coordination within a single provider or orchestration layer [@arxiv:2308.08155; @arxiv:2308.00352; @arxiv:2307.07924]. Recent systems move toward semi-autonomous scientific discovery [@arxiv:2505.13400], while domain-specific multi-agent workflows are being developed for scientific data access and analysis [@arxiv:2503.05854]. This work sits deliberately at the low-autonomy end of that spectrum. Manubot supplies the reproducible manuscript pipeline used here [@doi:10.1371/journal.pcbi.1007128].
