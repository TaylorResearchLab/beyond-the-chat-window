# Introduction

This work aims to help scientists adopt collaborative AI while retaining intellectual and process control of their research and maintaining a rigorous scientific record that supports reproducibility. The human investigator, termed the User, directs the scientific process. The workflow connects AI contributions to documented decisions, methods, evidence, and revisions so that work can be examined, reproduced, and continued across sessions.

A scientific project may continue across many conversations while analysis, code development, validation, and writing proceed in parallel. The practical requirement addressed here is to connect each bounded contribution to the accepted question, relevant upstream decisions, exact artifacts, and evidence that govern the project. This requirement has established precedents in reproducible computation, provenance, human-computer interaction, and collaborative AI. The present work brings these strands together as an operating workflow for scientists using separate interactive AI services.

## Foundations in reproducible research and human-directed collaboration

Sandve and colleagues' *Ten Simple Rules for Reproducible Computational Research* requires researchers to preserve how results were produced, record program versions and parameters, version-control scripts, and connect manuscript statements to underlying results [@doi:10.1371/journal.pcbi.1003285]. The W3C PROV-DM recommendation, edited by Moreau and Missier, provides a complementary data model for entities, activities, responsible agents, and derivation relationships [@url:https://www.w3.org/TR/2013/REC-prov-dm-20130430/]. These works supply precedents for linking an AI-assisted interpretation to its source files, execution record, and revision history. The operational records described here are not presented as an implementation of the PROV data model.

Wilkinson and colleagues articulated the Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable (FAIR) principles for digital research objects, emphasizing identifiers, metadata, access mechanisms, and provenance [@doi:10.1038/sdata.2016.18]. Himmelstein and colleagues' Manubot connects collaborative manuscript writing to version control, reviewable changes, automated document generation, and identifier-based citation handling [@doi:10.1371/journal.pcbi.1007128]. Our manuscript uses Manubot, while the collaboration record links scientific decisions to the artifacts being developed. The FAIR assessment concerns documented support for these principles rather than certification of the workflow or its products.

Human direction also has a substantial prior literature. Horvitz's principles for mixed-initiative interfaces address uncertainty about user goals, opportunities for direct invocation and termination, dialogue, and continued user refinement of automated work [@doi:10.1145/302979.303030]. In the specific setting of technical services, Wulf, Meierhofer, and Hannich distinguish interaction modes involving mandatory approval from modes involving escalation under uncertainty [@arxiv:2507.14034]. These precedents make human participation a design dimension rather than a feature unique to the present workflow. Here, the User's responsibility includes choosing the scientific question, directing attention across workstreams, and revising the project as evidence changes.

Shared-state coordination has an earlier systems lineage. Erman, Hayes-Roth, Lesser, and Reddy described Hearsay-II's use of specialized knowledge sources operating on a shared blackboard of hypotheses [@doi:10.1145/356810.356816]. Nii subsequently distinguished the blackboard problem-solving model from particular control architectures [@doi:10.1609/aimag.v7i2.537]. A common record through which participants contribute and inspect partial work is therefore an established architectural pattern. Our use of a Collaboration Log applies that pattern to a scientist-directed operating process; it does not introduce a new blackboard architecture.
